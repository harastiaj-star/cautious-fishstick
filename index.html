Design tools for people who make things by hand

Trailhead is a free set of tools for posters, zines, patches, trail maps and anything else you'd rather look printed than digital. Pick colors from a photo, turn a snapshot into a linocut or a topo map, lay out a zine that folds from one sheet of paper, then take it all into Photoshop with the layers intact.

Everything runs in your browser. Photos you open stay on your device, and there's nothing to sign up for.

Trailhead for Photoshop

A free panel for Photoshop. It runs Linocut, Photo to topo, Line art and Cyanotype on the photo you have open, and puts the result on a new layer. Its dither tool turns any photo into halftone dots, 1-bit pixels, riso grain or engraved lines in up to three inks, with an actual-size preview. It also opens files you save here straight from the zip, keeps all ten trail palettes a click away, pulls colors from your document, and paints paper grain, halftone and other textures.

Works with Photoshop 2023 or newer. Dither, textures and pulling colors need Photoshop 2024 or newer.

  1. 1

    Download and unzip

    You get Trailhead.ccx and a short README with these same steps.

  2. 2

    Double-click Trailhead.ccx

    Quit Photoshop first. The Creative Cloud app opens and warns that the plugin isn't verified by Adobe yet. Click Install locally, then OK.

  3. 3

    Open it in Photoshop

    Choose Plugins › Trailhead. Drag the panel into your dock to keep it there.

  4. 4

    If the install fails

    Some versions of Photoshop 2026 refuse plugins installed this way. Install Adobe's free UXP Developer Tool, unzip Trailhead.ccx into a folder, then use Add Plugin and Load. The README walks through it.

Cyanotype

          
Turns a photo into a negative for sun printing. Print the negative on transparency film, lay it on paper coated with cyanotype chemistry, and leave it in the sun. The preview shows roughly how the finished print will look.

These times are rough starting points. Every paper, batch of chemistry and day is different, so make a test strip first: cover the print with card and slide it back a little every few minutes. When the time is up, rinse the print in cool water for about five minutes, until the water runs clear. The blue deepens as it dries over the next day.

The film sheet is a 300 dpi image at true size. Print it on inkjet transparency film at 100% with no scaling. Mirroring puts the ink side against the paper, which keeps the print sharp. The curve corrects for how cyanotype handles midtones. If your prints come out too dark in the middle, raise it. If they look flat, lower it.

Privacy

          
Last updated October 14, 2026.

        

        

          
Your photos and files stay with you

          
Every tool on this site runs inside your browser. When you open a photo, draw letters or build a poster, the work happens on your own device. The site does not upload your photos, drawings or designs to any server.

          
What gets saved on your device

          
To pick up where you left off, the site keeps a few things in your browser's local storage: your mood board, the letters you've drawn in Letter maker, your trail map, the last tool you had open, whether you picked light or dark mode, and your Pocket scanner pairing. They stay in this browser on this device. Nobody else can see them, including us.

          
To remove them, use Clear the board, Start over or Clear the map in each tool, or clear this site's data in your browser settings.

          
Downloads

          
Nothing saves to your computer until you press a save button and confirm the download.

          
Outside services

          
Two open-source code libraries load from the jsDelivr network when the page opens: ag-psd, which writes Photoshop files, and opentype.js, which builds fonts. Like any request on the web, jsDelivr receives your IP address and basic browser details when it sends them. Its practices are described in the jsDelivr privacy policy.

          
This page is hosted by Anthropic on claude.ai. Anthropic's handling of visits to claude.ai is covered by the Anthropic privacy policy.

          
Video

          
The Video tools play and edit your clip inside your browser. Clips aren't uploaded anywhere, and a saved video or GIF is made on your device.

          
Three tools that talk to other services

          
Living prints uses your phone's camera to find the print. The video stays on your phone and is never recorded or sent anywhere. The QR code on a living print holds the tool settings needed to rebuild the animation, and nothing else. The tracking library (MindAR) and the QR code library load from jsDelivr when you use the tool.

          
Lake color year asks NASA's Global Imagery Browse Services for small satellite images of the place and dates you pick. NASA receives those requests along with your IP address, as with any website. No photo of yours is involved.

          
Pocket scanner sends the photos you choose to send from your phone to your Photoshop panel. Before a photo leaves your phone it's encrypted with a key that only your phone and your panel have, then passed through ntfy.sh, a free relay service, which stores the locked file for up to three hours. Neither ntfy.sh nor this site can see the picture. The pairing is saved in your phone's browser until you press Unpair.

          
What the site doesn't do

          
No accounts, no ads, no analytics and no tracking cookies.

          
Changes and questions

          
If any of this changes, this page will be updated with a new date. Questions go to harastiaj@gmail.com.

Photoshop handoff

          
How to get your files from this site into Photoshop.

        

        

          
    
            
  1. 1
    Save it for Photoshop
    Press Save for Photoshop in any tool that has it, from Adjust to the Poster maker. In Palette, press Save swatches.
    2. 
            
  2. 2
    Open it with the Trailhead panel
    This site can't hand your browser a .psd or .ase file directly, so each one comes in a zip. If you have the Trailhead plugin, go to Plugins › Trailhead, press Open a Trailhead file and pick the zip. Without the plugin, double-click the zip to unpack it first.
    3. 
            
  3. 3
    Open the .psd
    Your original sits on the bottom layer with the edit above it. Lower the edit's opacity, add a mask, or hide it to compare. Blend modes stay editable.
    4. 
            
  4. 4
    Load your swatches
    The Trailhead panel adds them when you open a palette zip. To do it by hand, open the Swatches panel menu, choose Import Swatches and pick the .ase file.
    5. 
          

          

            
.psdfrom Adjust, Blend modes, Print effects and Mood board
A layered Photoshop document. Photo edits keep your photo's size, and a mood board comes in at 1600 × 1000 with each item on its own layer.

            
.asefrom Palette
Adobe swatches that also open in Illustrator and InDesign. The zip includes CSS and JSON copies too.

            
.pngfrom every photo tool and Gradient
A flat image, good for sharing or dropping straight into a layout.

            
.svgfrom Line art
Every line as a vector, so it scales to any size and opens in Illustrator.

            
.otffrom Letter maker
Your own font. Double-click to install it, then restart Photoshop to see it in the font list.

            
the trailhead panel
A Photoshop plugin that opens these zips directly, runs the Linocut, topo, line art and cyanotype filters on any photo you have open, dithers it into dots, pixels or engraved lines in up to three inks, keeps all ten palettes in a panel, paints textures onto new layers, and takes photos straight from your phone. Download it from the home page. Once it's installed, it appears under Plugins › Trailhead.

          

        

      


      
      

        

          
About Trailhead

        

        

          

            
Trailhead is a small toolkit for people who make things: posters, zines, trail maps, gig flyers, a logo.

            
Build a palette from a photo of a mountain lake. Size the type for a gig poster. Add film grain to a photo, or split it into two inks for a screen print. When it's ready, open it in Photoshop with the layers intact.

            
I'm a graphic design student at Michigan State University, and I live in Michigan. Most of what's here comes from time spent outside, in national parks and on Michigan's lakes. The rest comes from studying anthropology, which keeps me interested in how people make things by hand.
